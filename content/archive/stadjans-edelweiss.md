> “It all began in the late 1970s when an Italian-born man named Ido Poloni wanted to bring with him to Sweden a dear memory from his homeland, the fabled edelweiss plant. After a summer stay at Foskdalsvallen in Särna, he found it natural to choose Dalarna's perhaps most alpine location for the planting attempt, namely Städjan's peak. Bringing about 20 plants from Cortina in northern Italy, Ido planted edelweiss on suitable rocks that resembled their natural habitat. Here the noble flower also found itself at home and is still growing on some of Städjan's rock ledges.” —*Falu-Kuriren, 1978*

Our search for Ido Poloni’s transplanted Italian edelweiss led to a field trip to the Städjan-Nipfjället national reserve in west Sweden, on traditional Sámi territory. Commonly treasured as a symbol of alpine nationalism and heroic mountaineering, the flower here appears as an ecological and cultural intruder, raising questions about conservation and belonging.

It is easy to portray the planting as a romantic folly at odds with bureaucratic order. But what if the state is the one clinging to a romantic idea of nature? The 1970s environmental movement, and the policies it shaped, leaned on protectionism and the notion of pristine ecosystems. As journalist Fred Pearce notes in *The New Wild*: “Most of us are appalled when foreign humans are treated as somehow intrinsically dangerous. Yet the orthodoxy in conservation is to demonize foreign species in just that way.”

Change, rather than stability, is the baseline. Alien species often integrate into ecosystems or fade away. The edelweiss on Mount Städjan seems to fall into the latter category.

After hours on the volcano-shaped summit we left without spotting the flower. Perhaps we missed the right rock, or perhaps the plants have already disappeared. It is plausible they were picked to extinction—an echo of how hikers once drove the Alpine edelweiss to protected status in 1878, in what was one of Europe’s first environmental protection laws.
